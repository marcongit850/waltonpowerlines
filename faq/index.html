FAQ

Common questions

Short answers about phased undergrounding in Walton County.

Would underground lines be more reliable?

On wind and falling trees, yes, for the spans that move. FPL says vegetation contact is a leading cause of outages in severe weather, and it reports that its underground neighborhood lines performed about five to 14 times better than overhead lines in the 2024 hurricane season, and about 50 percent better day to day. That is FPL’s comparison of FPL lines. It is context where FPL serves, including much of Miramar Beach and the Inlet Beach area. It is not a result for CHELCO lines and not a Walton study.

Duke Energy Florida’s targeted program was aimed at branch lines with a history of vegetation outages and lines that are hard for trucks to reach. The idea in both cases is to fix the lines that fail often, not to claim a perfect grid.

Will this keep the power on in a hurricane?

It can reduce outages caused by wind and debris on the lines that were buried. It cannot keep power on if the lines that feed the area are damaged, if a substation floods, or if the underground equipment itself is underwater.

Duke’s Florida storm plan treats flood risk to underground equipment as its own job, separate from burying overhead lines. The California Public Utilities Commission also notes that underground lines avoid some wind damage and that crews often need longer to find a buried fault. Both statements can be true.

Can underground lines still fail?

Yes. Dig-ins, water, failed splices, and equipment problems still cause outages. Restoration can take longer because the damage is harder to see. Undergrounding trades one set of problems for a smaller, different set. It does not repeal outages.

How much would it cost?

This site does not have a Walton County cost. No corridor here has been engineered or bid.

For scale, and only for scale: a 2005 Florida Public Service Commission preliminary analysis estimated $94.5 billion to underground the overhead distribution lines and feeders of the state’s five investor-owned utilities. That number is old, statewide, and mostly an inflation update of a 1991 study. It does not include cooperatives such as CHELCO, and it is not a price for 30A. A real local number would come from a feasibility study.

Who pays?

No one has decided. Options a study could examine include county funds, a utility’s own storm program, grants, special assessments, an MSTU or MSBU, developer payments on new construction, and doing the work during a road project. They are described on the cost and funding page. None is proposed as your tax.

Where FPL serves, including much of Miramar Beach and the Inlet Beach area, FPL describes two answers. If FPL selects a neighborhood for its Storm Secure Underground Program, FPL says customers do not pay an upfront conversion bill; the cost is in the storm-protection charge on FPL bills. If a community requests a conversion on its own, FPL says that community pays. That is not how the whole county would pay. CHELCO serves much of Santa Rosa Beach, Freeport, and inland Walton, and this site does not describe a CHELCO undergrounding program.

Who owns the lines?

The electric utility owns its poles and wires. Service in Walton County is split. Miramar Beach and the Inlet Beach area are largely FPL’s Northwest Florida territory, formerly Gulf Power. Destin, just west of the county line, is in that same FPL territory. Gulf Power merged into FPL on January 1, 2021. CHELCO, the member-owned cooperative, says it serves most of Walton County, including much of Santa Rosa Beach, Freeport, and inland Walton. Scenic Highway 30A crosses both. This site does not have a block-by-block ownership map. The name on a power bill is the check for one address. Cable and phone companies own their own wires, even when those wires hang on an electric pole.

Would cable and internet move too?

Only if those companies join the project. Poles often carry electric, phone, and cable together. If the power line moves and the others stay, the pole stays. The Town of Palm Beach’s town-wide project was built to move the utilities on the poles, not the power line alone. A Walton study should ask the communications companies at the start.

When do the poles come down?

After the new system is in service and every attachment has left the pole. That can be months after the conduit is in the ground. Palm Beach’s September 2026 project notes still list pole and wire removal as its own step, separate from drilling and conduit work.

How long would this take?

No timeline is offered for Walton County. Elsewhere, this is multi-year work. Palm Beach has run a town-wide program in numbered phases; the town has described Phase 8 as the last phase, and town updates in 2026 still list active construction. On FPL’s own system, FPL’s neighborhood program is an ongoing part of a 10-year storm plan, not a one-season campaign and not a Walton schedule. A Walton feasibility study would be the first dated step, and this site does not set that date either.

Will the road be torn up?

In places, yes. Expect sections of shoulder or pavement to open, lane shifts, and boring pits. Also expect long stretches where you see little, because boring can pass under a driveway. The honest picture is uneven disruption, block by block, not a countywide closure and not a project you would not notice.

What are the boxes that remain?

They are transformers and switches that used to sit on the pole. They need air, access, and a pad. On FPL projects, FPL describes a typical equipment easement of about 10 by 10 feet, with the size varying by site. That is FPL’s description, not a CHELCO or Walton design. You would still see equipment. You would see fewer wires overhead.

Does the whole county have to be done?

No. The idea on this site is gradual. Some Florida utilities have undergrounded selected laterals, not every mile. CenterPoint’s June 2025 Houston settlement targets freeway crossings, hard-to-reach spots, and some critical customers, not the entire city. The Texas commission later adopted an order in that case. Neither document is a Walton plan. A Walton effort could stop after one corridor if the study or the first project does not justify a second.

Is this an official plan?

No. Walton Power Lines is a civic education site. It is not Walton County, not CHELCO, not FPL, and not an adopted policy, timeline, or budget.

Can I request my street?

You can tell the utility that serves your address, and the county, that you are interested. Where FPL serves, FPL says a community can request a voluntary conversion and that the community pays the conversion cost. This site does not describe an equivalent CHELCO offer. Being interested does not put a street on a construction list. The useful countywide step is a feasibility study of a defined corridor, not a pile of one-off requests.
